How to Ask for a Lower Interest Rate

A five-minute phone call that costs nothing to try, and can meaningfully speed up a payoff plan if it works.

Credit card interest rates aren't always fixed in the way they seem. Issuers sometimes lower a rate for an existing customer who asks — especially one who's been paying on time — because keeping a paying customer is usually worth more to them than losing one over a rate they'd have negotiated anyway. It doesn't always work, but it costs nothing but a phone call to find out.

Before you call

  • Know your payment history. On-time payments for the last several months (ideally 6–12) is the single strongest thing you have going for you.
  • Know your current rate and roughly what competing cards are offering — you don't need exact numbers, just a sense of the market.
  • Know if you have other offers. A lower-rate offer from another card, even one you don't intend to use, is genuine leverage.

What to actually say

You don't need a complicated script — a direct, polite request works:

"Hi, I've been a customer for [X years] and have made my payments on time. I'm calling to ask if you're able to lower the interest rate on my account. I've seen other cards offering lower rates and would like to stay with you if possible."

If the first representative says no, it's reasonable to politely ask if there's a retention or loyalty department that handles this — a common recommendation, since some issuers route rate requests to a specific team.

What improves your odds

  • A consistent on-time payment history with that issuer.
  • A credit score that's improved since you opened the account.
  • Being a longer-tenured customer.
  • Carrying a meaningful balance — issuers have more incentive to keep a paying balance than to keep an account that's rarely used.

What to do if they say no

  • Ask about a promotional rate instead of a permanent one — some issuers offer a temporary lower rate even when they won't commit to a permanent change.
  • Ask what it would take — sometimes a representative will say a rate review happens after a certain number of on-time payments, giving you a concrete date to try again.
  • Consider a balance transfer or consolidation instead — see debt consolidation vs. the snowball method for how that compares.
  • Try again in a few months. A "no" today doesn't mean "no" permanently, especially if your payment history keeps improving.

If it works

A lower rate on a debt you're actively paying down changes your numbers immediately — less of each payment goes to interest, more reduces the balance.
