What Is the Debt Snowball Method?

A plain-language walkthrough of how the snowball method works, why the payoff order matters, and how to try it with your own numbers.

By M.T., PayoffSnowball editor · Published September 17, 2026 · Updated October 5, 2026 · How we research

The debt snowball method is a way of paying off multiple debts by focusing extra money on the debt with the smallest balance first, regardless of its interest rate, while paying the minimum on everything else. Once that smallest debt is gone, its payment rolls into the next-smallest one, and the amount you're able to throw at each remaining debt grows — like a snowball rolling downhill.

How the debt snowball method works

  1. List every debt you owe — credit cards, car loans, personal loans, medical bills — with its balance, interest rate, and minimum payment.
  2. Sort the list by balance, smallest to largest. Interest rate doesn't matter for this step.
  3. Pay the minimum on every debt, every month, without fail.
  4. Put every spare dollar toward the smallest debt until it's completely paid off.
  5. Roll that payment forward. The money that used to go to the paid-off debt — its minimum payment plus whatever extra you were adding — now goes to the next-smallest debt.
  6. Repeat until every debt is gone. Each payoff makes the next one faster, because the amount available to attack it keeps growing.

A simple example

Say you have three debts:

  • A $500 store card at 24% APR, $25 minimum
  • A $4,000 credit card at 19% APR, $100 minimum
  • A $12,000 car loan at 6% APR, $250 minimum

With $150 a month in extra money, the snowball method attacks the store card first because it's the smallest balance. Here it's also the highest-rate debt, so snowball and avalanche happen to agree. Once the store card is gone (month 3), its $25 minimum plus the $150 extra, $175 in total, rolls into the credit card. Once that's paid off (month 20), everything rolls into the car loan, and you're debt-free in month 36, paying $2,098 in total interest. With minimum payments only, the same debts take 64 months and cost $4,288 in interest. The $150 a month, plus rolling each paid-off minimum forward, saves about $2,190 and more than two years.

Why the order matters

Mathematically, paying off the highest-interest debt first (the debt avalanche method) usually saves more in total interest. The snowball method trades a small amount of that savings for something else: momentum. Paying off a whole debt — closing the account, seeing one fewer bill each month — is a concrete win that shows up early, which makes it easier to stick with a payoff plan over months or years. For a lot of people, finishing something is more motivating than shaving a percentage point off the total cost.

When the snowball method makes sense

  • You have several small debts and a couple of large ones, and you want early wins to stay motivated.
  • You've struggled to stick with a debt payoff plan before and need visible progress to keep going.
  • Your debts don't have wildly different interest rates, so the extra interest cost of snowballing over avalanching is small.

If your interest rates vary a lot — for example, a high-rate credit card next to a low-rate auto loan — it's worth checking both methods side by side before committing. Common mistakes

  • Skipping minimum payments on other debts to feed the smallest one. Late or missed minimums can trigger fees and hurt your credit — always pay every minimum first.
  • Not rolling the payment forward. The snowball only works if you keep redirecting freed-up money to the next debt instead of letting it disappear into everyday spending.
  • Ignoring debts that can never be paid off at the current rate — if a minimum payment doesn't even cover a month's interest, the balance grows no matter what. The calculator flags this automatically.
