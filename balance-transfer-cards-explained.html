0% APR Balance Transfer Cards, Explained

A real tool for cutting interest — with a deadline and a fee that decide whether it actually helps.

A balance transfer card lets you move an existing credit card balance onto a new card, often at a promotional 0% (or very low) interest rate for a limited introductory period — commonly somewhere in the 12–21 month range, though offers vary. During that window, your payments go almost entirely toward the balance instead of interest. After it ends, the rate jumps to the card's standard rate, which can be high.

The two numbers that decide if it's worth it

  • The transfer fee — usually a percentage of the balance transferred, commonly in the 3–5% range. A $5,000 transfer at 3% costs $150 upfront, which needs to be weighed against the interest you'd save.
  • The promotional period length — this sets your deadline. If you can't realistically pay off the balance before it ends, some or all of the benefit disappears once the standard rate applies to whatever's left.

A worked example

Say you move a $5,000 balance from a 22% APR card to a balance transfer card with a 15-month 0% promotional period and a 3% transfer fee ($150). If you pay it off in exactly 15 months, you pay $150 total in fees and $0 in interest during the promotional period — versus roughly what you'd have paid in interest at 22% over the same 15 months on the original card. Run your own balance and timeline through the calculator by temporarily setting the interest rate to match your plan (0% during the promo, then the standard rate as a separate scenario) to see the real comparison for your numbers.

What catches people out

  • Missing the deadline. Whatever balance remains when the promotional period ends starts accruing interest at the standard rate — often just as high as, or higher than, the card you transferred from.
  • Missing a payment. Many promotional offers include a clause that ends the 0% rate early if you miss a payment — read the terms before assuming the rate is guaranteed no matter what.
  • New spending on the old card. If you transfer a balance but don't stop using the original card, you can end up paying down the transferred balance while quietly rebuilding a new one — the classic consolidation trap covered in debt consolidation vs. the snowball method.
  • Assuming the whole balance transfers instantly. Transfers can take days to weeks to process — keep paying the original card until you confirm the transfer went through.

Using one inside a payoff plan

A balance transfer doesn't change your overall strategy — it changes one debt's interest rate for a limited time. If you use one, update that debt's rate to 0% (or whatever the promotional rate is) in the calculator, and treat the promotional period's end date as a hard deadline: aim to have that specific balance paid off, or close to it, before the rate reverts. Whether the snowball or avalanche method ranks it first or last during that window depends on the same balance/rate logic as any other debt — see snowball vs. avalanche for how that ranking works.
