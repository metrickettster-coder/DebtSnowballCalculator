Debt Snowball Spreadsheet vs. Calculator

What a spreadsheet does well, the mistake that quietly breaks most snowball spreadsheets, and when a calculator saves you time.

By M.T., PayoffSnowball editor · Published October 7, 2026 · Updated October 7, 2026 · How we research

Use a calculator to build the plan and a spreadsheet (or a notebook) to track what you actually paid. A calculator gets the math right in seconds, including the part most homemade spreadsheets get wrong. A spreadsheet is great for logging real payments and keeping you engaged month to month.

What a spreadsheet does well

  • It's yours. You can add columns for anything: due dates, account notes, a "paid" checkbox.
  • It tracks reality. Real life drifts from any plan. A spreadsheet is a good place to record what you really paid and what each statement really says.
  • It keeps you involved. Updating it every month is a small habit that helps some people stay motivated.

Where spreadsheets go wrong

Building a correct month-by-month snowball in a spreadsheet is harder than it looks. Each month you have to add interest to every balance, pay every minimum, send the extra money to the right debt, and spill any leftover onto the next debt when one hits zero. The most common mistake is the rollover.

When a debt is paid off, its minimum payment should keep going to the next debt every month from then on. That growing payment is the "snowball." A spreadsheet that forgets this, or only rolls it over once, makes your plan look slower and more expensive than it is.

How much the rollover changes the answer

Here's a three-debt example with $150 a month extra, run through our calculator with rollover on and off:

DebtBalanceAPRMinimum
Card A$90024%$35
Card B$3,40020%$90
Personal loan$7,50012%$220
  • With rollover: debt-free in month 29, about $2,120 in interest. By month 20 the personal loan is getting $495 a month.
  • Without rollover: debt-free in month 33, about $2,295 in interest.

Forgetting the rollover here adds 4 months and about $175. With bigger debts or more of them, the gap grows. The calculator does the rollover for you, and it has a switch to turn it off if you'd rather put freed-up minimums back in your budget.

Other things that are hard in a spreadsheet

  • Comparing orders. Trying snowball vs avalanche in a spreadsheet often means building it twice. The calculator has a slider.
  • A debt that never shrinks. If a minimum payment is less than a month's interest, the balance grows. The calculator flags it. Most spreadsheets won't. See the minimum payment trap.
  • "What if" questions. A tax refund lump sum, new charges on one card, or $50 more a month each take a few clicks in the calculator.

Use both: a simple routine

  1. Enter your debts in the calculator and find a monthly extra amount that works.
  2. Use the "What to pay each month" plan as your target. The Export CSV button saves each month's projected balances, which you can open in Excel or Google Sheets.
  3. Each month, log what you actually paid in your spreadsheet.
  4. Every few months, re-enter your real balances in the calculator to refresh your debt-free date.
