The Minimum Payment Trap

Why paying just the minimum can keep a balance alive for decades — and how to tell if one of your debts is stuck.

Credit card statements list a minimum payment — usually a small percentage of your balance, or a flat dollar amount, whichever is higher. Paying it keeps the account in good standing. What it doesn't do, in a lot of cases, is make meaningful progress on the balance.

How minimum payments are calculated

Most issuers set the minimum payment as roughly 1–3% of the balance, with a floor (often $25–$35). As the balance shrinks, so does the required minimum — which is exactly backwards from what you'd want if the goal is to actually pay the debt off. Early on, a large share of that minimum payment goes toward interest, and a much smaller share reduces the principal.

A worked example

Take a $5,000 balance at 22% APR with a 2% minimum payment ($100 to start, shrinking as the balance drops). Paying only that shrinking minimum:

  • Roughly $92 of the first $100 payment goes to interest — only about $8 reduces the balance.
  • As the balance slowly drops, the required minimum drops too, so the pace of payoff barely accelerates.
  • The balance can take well over a decade to clear this way, and the total interest paid can end up close to or higher than the original balance.

Run your own balance and rate through the calculator to see the actual numbers instead of an estimate — the difference between the minimum and even a modest extra payment is usually dramatic.

When a debt is truly stuck

There's a specific, checkable condition for a debt that will never shrink at its current payment: if the minimum payment doesn't exceed one month's interest, the balance grows every month no matter how long you pay. For a $5,000 balance at 22% APR, one month's interest alone is about $92 — a minimum payment below that means the balance increases even though you're paying on time, every month.

This isn't rare. It happens most often on high-rate store cards and cards near their limit, where minimum payment floors haven't kept pace with the balance or rate. The calculator checks this automatically for every debt you enter and flags it, so you don't have to do the arithmetic by hand.

Getting out of the trap

  • Pay any fixed amount above the minimum, even $20–$50 a month, and principal starts shrinking meaningfully faster — because unlike the minimum, a fixed extra payment doesn't shrink along with the balance.
  • Use the snowball or avalanche method once you have more than one debt, so extra payments are always directed somewhere instead of spread thin.
  • Check the math on each debt rather than assuming the minimum is doing its job — a debt can look like it's being paid down while the balance quietly climbs.
