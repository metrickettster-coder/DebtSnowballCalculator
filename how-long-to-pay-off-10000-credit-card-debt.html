How Long to Pay Off $10,000 in Credit Card Debt

Payoff times and total interest for $10,000 at different monthly payments, so you can pick a number and see where it leads.

By M.T., PayoffSnowball editor · Published October 7, 2026 · Updated October 8, 2026 · How we research

The short answer: at 22% APR, paying a fixed $300 a month clears $10,000 in 52 months (about 4 years and 4 months) and costs about $5,596 in interest. Paying $500 a month cuts that to 26 months and about $2,571. The monthly payment you choose matters more than anything else.

Why $200 a month takes over 11 years

At 22% APR, one month of interest on $10,000 is about $183. A $200 payment leaves only about $17 to shrink the balance in the first month. That's why the $200 row costs more in interest ($17,356) than the debt itself. Going from $200 to $250 a month, just $50 more, cuts the time roughly in half and saves over $9,000.

The lesson: the first dollars above one month's interest do the most work. If your payment is close to your monthly interest, any small raise helps a lot. Our guide to the minimum payment trap covers what happens when the payment drops below that line.

Owe a different amount? See how long it takes to pay off $5,000 or $20,000 in credit card debt. At the same rate, the months line up whenever the payment is the same share of the balance: $150 on $5,000, $300 on $10,000 and $600 on $20,000 each take 52 months.

What if your rate is different?

Same $10,000, same approach, two other rates:

  • 18% APR: $300 a month takes 47 months and about $3,967 in interest. $500 a month takes 24 months and about $1,978.
  • 29% APR: $300 a month takes 69 months and about $10,574 in interest. $500 a month takes 28 months and about $3,828.

A higher rate punishes small payments much more than big ones. At $500 a month the gap between 18% and 29% is about $1,850. At $300 a month it's about $6,600. If you can lower the rate, our guides on asking for a lower rate and balance transfer cards explain the options and the catches.

Fixed payment vs. the minimum

The table uses a fixed payment that stays the same every month. A card's required minimum usually shrinks as the balance shrinks, which stretches payoff out even further. If you only pay the minimum, your real timeline is longer than any row above. Picking a fixed number and sticking to it is the simplest way to stay on the fast side of this table.

If $10,000 is spread across several cards

Many people don't owe $10,000 on one card. They owe it across three or four. The total time is driven by the same thing (how much you pay each month in total), but the order you pay them in changes the interest and how soon you close your first account. The debt snowball pays the smallest balance first. The avalanche pays the highest rate first. In our 10,000-plan study, the extra payment amount mattered far more than which order you picked.

Find your own number

  1. Look up your balance, APR and minimum payment on your latest statement.
  2. Enter them in the calculator and set the extra amount you can pay each month.
  3. Move the extra amount up and down by $25 or $50 and watch your debt-free date and total interest change.
