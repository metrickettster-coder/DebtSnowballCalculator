Using the Debt Snowball Method With Student Loans

Student loans behave differently from credit cards in a few ways that matter for how you sequence a payoff plan.

The debt snowball and avalanche methods work the same way regardless of debt type — smallest balance first, or highest rate first. But student loans have a few quirks worth knowing before you plug them into a payoff plan alongside credit cards or other loans.

What makes student loans different

  • Multiple loans, one "debt." Many borrowers have several individual student loans (sometimes from the same servicer, sometimes split across subsidized/unsubsidized or multiple years of borrowing). You can treat each loan as its own entry in the calculator, or combine them if they share the same rate and you don't need the individual breakdown.
  • Often lower rates than credit cards. Federal student loan rates are typically well below typical credit card APRs, which usually means they rank lower priority under the avalanche method — but the snowball method may still put a small student loan early if its balance is small, regardless of its (comparatively low) rate.
  • Income-driven repayment plans can change your minimum payment based on income, which affects what "minimum payment" even means for that loan — use your current required payment as the minimum in the calculator, and update it if your plan recalculates.
  • Deferment and forbearance pause payments but interest often keeps accruing (depending on loan type) — a paused loan isn't a zero-cost loan.

Should student loans go first or last?

There's no universal answer — it depends on the rate relative to your other debts:

  • If a student loan's rate is lower than your credit cards, the avalanche method naturally puts it last, which is usually the mathematically efficient order.
  • If it's your smallest balance, the snowball method puts it first regardless of rate — reasonable if the early win matters more to you than a small amount of extra interest.
  • A blended approach (see the debt snowball method and snowball vs. avalanche) lets you weigh both factors instead of picking one extreme.

A common scenario: high-rate credit cards plus low-rate student loans

This is one of the clearest cases where avalanche and snowball can genuinely diverge, because the gap between a low student loan rate and a high credit card rate is often large. Enter both into the calculator and check the comparison table — you'll typically see avalanche save more in total interest here specifically because that rate gap is wide, which is exactly the scenario where the choice of method matters most.

Don't forget loan forgiveness programs

If you're pursuing a forgiveness program (for example, one tied to public service employment) that forgives a remaining balance after a set number of qualifying payments, aggressively prepaying that specific loan may work against you — you'd be reducing a balance that was going to be forgiven anyway instead of directing that money toward debt that won't be. Check your program's rules before treating a forgiveness-track loan the same as your other debts in a payoff plan.
