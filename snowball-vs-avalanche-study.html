How Much Does the Debt Snowball Really Cost? We Ran 10,000 Payoff Plans

Most articles say the avalanche method "saves more money." That's true, but it doesn't say how much. We measured it.

By M.T., PayoffSnowball editor · Published October 5, 2026 · How we research

Key findings

  • Across 10,000 simulated households, the snowball method cost a median of $190 more in interest than the avalanche method. That's about 0.5% of the total debt.
  • In 24% of cases the two methods cost exactly the same, because the smallest debt was also the most expensive one. In 40% the gap was under $100.
  • The snowball delivered the first paid-off debt in a median of 10 months, compared with 16 months for avalanche. In the first year, households cleared 0.82 debts on average with snowball and 0.50 with avalanche.
  • The debt-free date was the same month in 72% of cases and within one month in 97%.
  • The gap gets bigger as interest rates spread further apart and as you juggle more debts. That's when it's worth running your own numbers.

Why we ran this

The snowball-versus-avalanche debate usually ends with two half-answers: "avalanche is mathematically optimal" and "snowball is psychologically better." Both are true, and neither helps you decide. The real question is what the motivation costs. If the snowball costs $40 over three years, almost anyone should take the early wins. If it costs $4,000, that's a different conversation.

Academic work backs up the motivation side. Researchers writing in Harvard Business Review found that people who focus on paying off one balance at a time feel more progress and are more likely to keep going. But we couldn't find a clear, published answer to the cost side, so we built one with our own calculator engine.

How we did it

We generated 10,000 realistic household debt profiles and ran each one through the exact same payoff engine the calculator uses, once in pure snowball mode and once in pure avalanche mode. Each profile had:

  • 2 to 6 debts, drawn from six common types: credit cards (17.99%–29.99% APR), store cards (25%–32%), personal loans (8%–24%), car loans (4%–14%), federal-style student loans (3.5%–8.5%), and 0% medical payment plans.
  • Balances and minimum payments typical of each type. Card minimums were set to 1% of the balance plus that month's interest (a common issuer formula). Loan payments came from standard amortization over typical terms.
  • An extra monthly payment of $50, $100, $200, $300, or $500, split evenly across the sample.

The median household in the sample owed $37,917 in total. Every simulation reached a payoff. The engine adds monthly interest, pays every minimum, sends the extra payment to the top-priority debt, and rolls each paid-off debt's minimum into the next one. How your debt-free date is calculated walks through that loop step by step.

Over a payoff that usually takes several years, a median of $190 works out to roughly $5 a month. For most households, that's the real price of the snowball's early wins. About one household in fifty paid more than $2,000 extra, though, so a small typical cost doesn't mean it's always small.

In about 2% of profiles, the snowball actually came out slightly cheaper. That happens because paying off a small debt early frees its minimum payment sooner, and the bigger rolling payment can occasionally make up for the higher-rate balance waiting a little longer. It's rare, but it shows why running the numbers beats following a rule of thumb.

The size of your extra payment matters less than you'd expect. The median gap stayed between about $125 and $245 across every extra-payment level we tested. A bigger extra payment shortens the payoff, which leaves less time for the order to matter, but it also moves more money through the "wrong" debt early on. The two effects roughly cancel out.

What this means for you

  • If your rates are within about 10 points of each other (say, several cards between 20% and 29%), pick whichever method you'll actually stick with. The typical cost difference is under $100.
  • If you have a very high-rate card next to low-rate loans, the snowball's cost can climb into four figures. Consider a blend: the calculator's slider ranks debts by both balance and rate, so you can keep some early wins without paying full price.
  • If you've struggled to stay on a plan before, the evidence points toward the snowball. Quitting halfway costs far more than a few hundred dollars of extra interest. How to stay motivated paying off debt covers other ways to protect your momentum.
  • Whatever you pick, the extra payment matters most. In our worked example on what the debt snowball is, adding $200 a month cut 25 months and about $2,840 of interest off a minimum-payments-only plan. That's more than nine times the cost of choosing snowball over avalanche.

Limitations

We want to be clear about what this study is and isn't:

  • The profiles are simulated, not real people's accounts. Balances, rates, and terms were drawn from ranges typical of each debt type. They aren't a statistical sample of U.S. households. For real-world context, the Federal Reserve's G.19 consumer credit report tracks total revolving and non-revolving consumer debt.
  • Rates and payments are held fixed. Real card APRs are often variable, promotional rates expire, and minimums change as balances fall. The calculator models fixed rates and fixed minimums.
  • It assumes perfect follow-through. The whole point of the snowball is that people aren't perfect. A study that assumes everyone sticks to the plan will always favor avalanche. Treat the numbers above as the cost of the snowball if you'd have followed avalanche perfectly, which most people don't.
  • This is not financial advice. It's a measurement of how two payoff orders compare. Your own numbers are what count. See our terms.
